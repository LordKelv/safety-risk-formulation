# Psychiatric Emergency Risk Assessment Literature Synthesis: Suicide & Interpersonal Violence

**Target Audience & Setting:** Emergency psychiatry clinicians, residents, and medical trainees evaluating adults in psychiatric emergency services (PES / CPEP) and acute behavioral health settings.  
**Methodology & Verification Standard:** All relative risk metrics (RR, OR, HR, IRR, SMR), 95% confidence intervals (CIs), baseline rates, and citations below were extracted directly from peer-reviewed meta-analyses, umbrella reviews, nationwide registry cohorts, and official U.S. federal surveillance reports (CDC/NCHS, SAMHSA, VA, BJS/FBI). Where a primary source did not compute a formal relative ratio or 95% CI (e.g., full-population census vital statistics ratios, forensic base-rate cohorts, or continuous-scale meta-analyses), the missing field is explicitly recorded as `null` and explained in the notes rather than interpolated or fabricated.

## 3. Guidance on Combining Risk Factors & Predictive Limitations

### A. Why Multiplying Relative Risks Overstates Risk (Mathematical & Causal Pitfalls)
A central educational message for any clinician risk tool is that **univariable relative risks cannot be multiplied together ($\text{RR}_{\text{total}} \neq \prod \text{RR}_i$)** for four reasons:
1. **Severe Non-Independence (Collinearity & Shared Variance):** Psychiatric risk factors cluster densely. For example, BPD, ≥4 ACEs, AUD, prior suicide attempts, hopelessness, and recent relationship rupture share underlying causal pathways. In multivariable models, entering correlated predictors dramatically attenuates individual ORs:
   * *Example 1 (Suicide — PTSD & Prior Attempts):* In Fox et al.'s (2021) cohort of 3.1 million Swedes, female PTSD suicide HR dropped from **6.74** (demographic-adjusted) to **2.61** after adjusting for psychiatric comorbidity and prior attempts (~57% of excess risk was shared/mediated).
   * *Example 2 (Violence — Bipolar/Schizophrenia & SUD):* In Fazel et al. (2010), bipolar violence OR dropped from **6.4** (with SUD) to **1.3** (without SUD) and **1.1** in sibling controls; naively multiplying Bipolar (OR 4.1) × SUD (OR 7.4) × Male (OR 3.0) = 91x grossly double-counts shared variance.
2. **Baseline Denominator Mismatch:** Mixing general-population case-control ORs (e.g., MDD OR = 11.0 vs. healthy community controls) with clinical-sample dynamic ORs (e.g., C-SSRS intent OR = 3.26 vs. other suicidal patients; post-discharge SMR = 83) applies clinical multipliers on top of already-clinical base rates.
3. **Breakdown of the Rare-Disease Approximation at High Risk:** Odds ratios diverge sharply from risk ratios once predicted probability exceeds ~10%. Even in valid logistic models, probabilities follow $P = \frac{\text{Odds}}{1 + \text{Odds}}$, which naturally saturates below 1.0, whereas raw RR multiplication can absurdly exceed 100%.
4. **Collider & Over-Adjustment Paradoxes:** As demonstrated by Jiang, Fox, & Gradus (2021, *Psychol Trauma*, PMID: 33900782), conditioning on treatment-seeking status (e.g., ED presentation) or downstream mediators creates collider stratification bias that alters factor weights relative to the general population.

### B. Empirical Limits of Prediction: Franklin et al. (2017) & Large et al. (2016)
* **Franklin et al. (2017, *Psychol Bull*, 143(2):187–232, PMID: 27841450):** Meta-analyzed 365 longitudinal studies (3,428 effect sizes) across 50 years (1965–2014). Across all longitudinal predictors, the pooled weighted odds ratio was **wOR = 1.50 (95% CI 1.46–1.56)** for suicide death and **wOR = 1.51 (1.49–1.54; trim-and-fill adjusted = 1.26)** for suicide attempts. Predictive accuracy (AUC ~0.56–0.58) barely exceeded chance and **did not improve over 50 years**, demonstrating that isolated risk factors are weak longitudinal predictors over months to years.
* **Large et al. (2016, *PLoS One*, 11(6):e0156322, PMID: 27285387):** Meta-analyzed 37 longitudinal cohort studies (53 samples) of multi-factor psychiatric suicide risk stratification. Although "high-risk" patients had **OR = 4.84 (95% CI 3.79–6.20)** vs. "lower-risk" patients:
  * **Positive Predictive Value (PPV) was only 5.5% (95% CI 3.5%–8.5%)** over a mean follow-up of **63 months** (~94.5% of "high-risk" patients did *not* die by suicide, and short-term 30-day PPV is typically <0.5%–1%).
  * **Sensitivity was only 56% (95% CI 48%–64%)**—meaning **44% of all suicides occurred in patients classified as "low" or "moderate" risk**.
* **Fazel et al. (2012, *BMJ*, 345:e4692, PMID: 22833655):** Across 73 samples (N = 24,827) evaluating violence risk tools, discrimination was substantially stronger than for suicide (**median AUC = 0.72**, DOR = 6.1, NPV = **91%**, PPV = **41%** in forensic/offender samples), supporting their utility to screen out low-risk patients (high NPV) while cautioning against relying on risk scores alone for preventive detention.

### C. Published Multivariable Models to Anchor an Illustrative Combined Estimate
If the web app displays an **illustrative combined risk visual**, it should explicitly disclaim clinical prediction and model its behavior on validated, shrunken multivariable tools:
1. **OxMIS (Oxford Mental Illness and Suicide Tool — Fazel et al., 2019, *Transl Psychiatry*, 9:98, DOI: 10.1038/s41398-019-0428-3, PMID: 30770788):**
   * *Design:* 17-item multivariable logistic/survival model predicting **12-month suicide mortality** in schizophrenia-spectrum or bipolar disorder (N = 75,158 in Sweden; externally validated in Finland, N = 137,112).
   * *Key Calibration Feature for App Design:* Shrunk multivariable log-odds coefficients (where individual adjusted ORs mostly fall between 1.1 and 2.3 rather than unadjusted 10–16x), and **predicted 12-month suicide risk is explicitly capped at a 5% ceiling** to prevent implausible extrapolation. External AUC = **0.70–0.71**; at a 1% 1-year threshold: sensitivity = 55%, specificity = 75%, PPV = 2%, NPV = 99%.
2. **OxMIV (Oxford Mental Illness and Violence Tool — Fazel et al., 2017, *Lancet Psychiatry*, 4(6):461–468, DOI: 10.1016/S2215-0366(17)30109-8, PMID: 28479147):**
   * *Design:* 16-item multivariable model predicting **12-month violent crime conviction** in schizophrenia-spectrum or bipolar disorder (N = 75,158), **capped at a 20% 1-year ceiling**.
   * *Performance:* External validation **AUC = 0.89 (95% CI 0.85–0.93)**; at a 5% 1-year cutoff: sensitivity = 62%, specificity = 94%, PPV = 11%, NPV > 99%.
3. **OxRec (Oxford Risk of Recidivism Tool — Fazel et al., 2016, *Lancet Psychiatry*, 3(6):535–543, DOI: 10.1016/S2215-0366(16)00103-6, PMID: 27180024):**
   * *Design:* 14-item multivariable Cox model predicting 1- and 2-year violent reoffending post-prison release (N = 47,326). External C-index = **0.74**; 1-year PPV = 21%, NPV = 95%.
4. **VA REACH VET (McCarthy et al., 2015, *Am J Public Health*, PMID: 26180970; Matarazzo et al., 2023, *Psychiatr Serv*, PMID: 36039552; McCarthy et al., 2021, *JAMA Netw Open*, PMID: 34661663):**
   * *Design:* Regularized logistic regression across 61 VHA EHR variables. Veterans in the **top 0.1% predicted risk tier** exhibit **~30–40x concentration of suicide mortality** compared to the overall VHA population (top 5% accounts for ~24% of VHA suicides), yet absolute 1-year suicide mortality in the top 0.1% tier remains ~1.5%–2%—justifying proactive outreach, care review, and safety planning rather than coercive disposition alone.
5. **Recommended Pedagogical Formula if Illustrating Combined Risk in the App:**
   * Convert unadjusted general-population RRs to log-odds increments with a **collinearity attenuation / shrinkage damper** (e.g., damped additive log-odds $\ln(\text{Odds}) = \ln(\text{Odds}_0) + \sum_{k=1}^{m} \gamma^{k-1} \cdot \alpha \ln(\text{RR}_k)$ sorted by magnitude with $\alpha \approx 0.35\text{–}0.50$ and diminishing return $\gamma \approx 0.75$, or domain-grouped max/sub-additive pooling), convert back via $\frac{\text{Odds}}{1+\text{Odds}}$, and **enforce OxMIS/OxMIV-inspired ceilings** (e.g., max illustrative 1-year suicide death probability ~3%–5%; max 1-year serious violence probability ~25%–35%), paired with a visual icon array showing false positives vs. true positives.

## 4. Key Clinical Frameworks to Cite in the Web App

1. **SAFE-T (Suicide Assessment Five-Step Evaluation and Triage)**
   * *Citation:* Jacobs DG (Screening for Mental Health / SAMHSA, 2009; HHS Pub. No. SMA 09-4432).
   * *Clinical Role:* Standard PES/ED clinical structure endorsed by SAMHSA and The Joint Commission: **(1) Identify Risk Factors** (explicitly distinguishing *modifiable* dynamic factors from static history), **(2) Identify Protective Factors** (noting that protective factors may not counteract acute severe suicidality), **(3) Conduct Suicide Inquiry** (ideation, plan, behaviors, intent), **(4) Determine Risk Level / Intervention** (matching triage disposition and mitigation to modifiable drivers rather than checking a score), and **(5) Document** rationale, safety plan, and lethal means restriction.
2. **C-SSRS (Columbia–Suicide Severity Rating Scale)**
   * *Citation:* Posner K, Brown GK, Stanley B, et al. *Am J Psychiatry*. 2011;168(12):1266–1277. DOI: 10.1176/appi.ajp.2011.10111704 (PMID: 22193671).
   * *Clinical Role:* Gold-standard validated screening and severity assessment separating **suicidal ideation severity** (1 = wish to be dead; 2 = non-specific active thoughts; 3 = active thoughts with method, no intent; 4 = active thoughts with some intent, no specific plan; 5 = active thoughts with specific plan and intent), **ideation intensity**, and **suicidal behavior** (actual, interrupted, and aborted attempts + preparatory acts). Operationalizes Joint Commission screening/triage workflows (SAFE-T with C-SSRS).
3. **HCR-20 V3 (Historical-Clinical-Risk Management-20, Version 3)**
   * *Citation:* Douglas KS, Hart SD, Webster CD, Belfrage H. *HCR-20V3: Assessing Risk for Violence – User Guide*. Mental Health, Law, and Policy Institute, Simon Fraser University; 2013 (Overview: Douglas et al., 2014, *Int J Forensic Ment Health*, 13(2):93–108, DOI: 10.1080/14999013.2014.906519).
   * *Clinical Role:* Premier **Structured Professional Judgment (SPJ)** framework for adult violence risk: **10 Historical (static) items** (H1–H10: prior violence, other antisocial behavior, relationships, employment, substance use, major mental disorder, personality disorder, traumatic experiences, violent attitudes, treatment/supervision response), **5 Clinical (acute/dynamic) items** (C1–C5: insight, violent ideation/intent, symptoms of major mental disorder, instability, treatment/supervision response), and **5 Risk Management (future contextual) items** (R1–R5: professional services, living situation, personal support, treatment compliance, stress/coping). Crucially, V3 rates both **Presence** and individual **Relevance** and **prohibits numeric score summation**, guiding individualized risk formulation and scenario planning.
4. **The Joint Commission National Patient Safety Goal 15.01.01 (NPSG.15.01.01)**
   * *Citation:* The Joint Commission. *R3 Report Issue 18: National Patient Safety Goal for Suicide Prevention* (Effective July 1, 2019; updated 2020–2025).
   * *Clinical Role:* Regulatory standard across U.S. accredited hospitals and behavioral health settings comprising **7 Elements of Performance (EPs)**: environmental ligature mitigation (EP1), validated suicide screening (EP2), evidence-based risk assessment of positive screens (EP3), risk/mitigation documentation (EP4), written policies/training (EP5), **written collaborative discharge safety planning + lethal means restriction counseling + warm handoff** (EP6), and quality monitoring (EP7).
5. **Stanley-Brown Safety Planning Intervention (SPI)**
   * *Citation:* Stanley B, Brown GK. *Safety Planning Intervention: a brief intervention to mitigate suicide risk*. *Cogn Behav Pract*. 2012;19(2):256–264. DOI: 10.1016/j.cbpra.2011.01.001; Trial evidence: Stanley et al., 2018, *JAMA Psychiatry*, 75(9):894–900 (PMID: 29998307).
   * *Clinical Role:* Evidence-based 6-step collaborative brief intervention replacing discredited "no-suicide contracts" (which have zero empirical efficacy and create medico-legal false reassurance): **(1)** recognizing personal warning signs, **(2)** internal coping strategies, **(3)** people/settings providing distraction, **(4)** family/friends to ask for help, **(5)** professionals/crisis lines (988), and **(6)** making the environment safe (lethal means restriction).
6. **CALM (Counseling on Access to Lethal Means)**
   * *Citation:* Barber CW, Miller MJ. *Reducing a suicidal person's access to lethal means of suicide: a research agenda*. *Am J Prev Med*. 2014;47(3 Suppl 2):S264–S272. DOI: 10.1016/j.amepre.2014.05.028 (PMID: 25145749); Suicide Prevention Resource Center (SPRC) CALM Training.
   * *Clinical Role:* Teaches emergency clinicians **why** means restriction saves lives: suicidal crises are frequently brief (minutes to hours), method substitution rarely replaces a high-lethality method (~85–90% fatality for firearms) with an equally lethal one (<5% for overdose), and collaborative counseling with patients/families to temporarily store firearms off-site (or locked/disassembled with keys held by a trusted person) and limit lethal medication supplies directly prevents fatal outcomes.

## 5. Top 10 Figures With Lowest Confidence / Highest Methodological Uncertainty

To assist cross-model verification and transparent UI caveats in the teaching app, these are the **10 rows/figures we are least confident about translating directly into a uniform "vs. general population" RR display**:

1. **`command_hallucinations_suicide` (OR = 0.50, 95% CI 0.35–0.71; Hawton et al., 2005):** Hawton et al.'s pooled estimate measures *any* hallucinations among patients with schizophrenia (showing a paradoxical protective/inverse association likely due to confounding by preserved insight and depressive subtype in non-hallucinating suicides), rather than isolating *self-harm command hallucinations* vs. the general population. True acute RR of self-harm commands vs. general population is unknown.
2. **`chronic_pain_ipv_violence` (OR = 1.84, 95% CI 1.12–3.04; Crane & Easton, 2017):** No general-population meta-analysis exists quantifying chronic pain as an independent predictor of interpersonal violence; this OR comes from an offender AUD sample for IPV perpetration (and population analgesic-homicide linkages like Tiihonen et al., 2015 [opioid RR = 1.92] suffer from severe confounding by indication/antisocial injury).
3. **`psychopathy_pclr_violence` (`null` OR; meta-analytic *d* = 0.56–0.64; Yang et al., 2010 / Leistico et al., 2008):** PCL-R validation studies are conducted almost exclusively within forensic/correctional samples and report Cohen's *d* or AUC rather than general-population odds ratios; Coid & Yang's (2009/2011) British household PCL:SV survey had very small cell counts at clinical cutoffs ($N=638$ total sample).
4. **`opioid_use_disorder_suicide` (SMR = 13.5, `null` CI; Chesney et al., 2014):** Chesney et al.'s meta-review reports the point SMR from underlying specialist OUD cohorts without a pooled 95% CI, and forensic toxicology routinely struggles to separate intentional opioid suicides from unintentional/undetermined overdoses.
5. **`firearm_safe_storage_locked_suicide` (OR = 0.39, 95% CI 0.24–0.66; Shenassa et al., 2004 / OR = 0.27; Grossman et al., 2005):** The strongest safe-storage data come from youth/household-member access studies (Grossman et al., 2005) or older 1993 NMFS mortality followback surveys (Shenassa et al., 2004), where recent re-analyses (Miller et al., 2025) note substantial attenuation when an adult firearm owner personally controls the lock/combination.
6. **`reasons_for_living_attempt` (HR = 0.32, 95% CI 0.17–0.60; Lizardi et al., 2007):** Despite 39 supportive studies in Bakhiyi et al.'s (2016) systematic review, heterogeneity in RFLI scoring prevented a pooled meta-analytic OR; Lizardi et al.'s prospective HR of 0.32 held in depressed female inpatients but was non-significant in men (HR = 0.83).
7. **`recent_psych_discharge_3mo_suicide` (SMR ≈ 82.6, `null` pooled SMR CI; Chung et al., 2017):** The pooled absolute incidence (**1,132 per 100,000 PY; 95% CI 874–1,467**) is rock-solid across 100 studies, but converting it to a single SMR requires dividing by a chosen national/global baseline rate (e.g., 1,132 / 13.7 = 82.6x U.S. 2024 age-adjusted rate vs. ~100x global WHO rate), and heterogeneity across health systems is high ($I^2 = 98\%$).
8. **`recent_psych_discharge_10wk_violence` (RR ≈ 2.50, `null` CI; Steadman et al., 1998):** Derived from the Pittsburgh site ratio of 11.5% (discharged patients) vs. 4.6% (neighborhood controls) over 10 weeks in the MacArthur study; crucially, when patients with active substance abuse were excluded, the difference vanished (4.7% vs. 3.3%, NS), so treating "recent discharge" as an independent violence multiplier risks stigmatizing non-substance-using patients.
9. **`male_sex_homicide` (RR ≈ 7.3, `null` CI; FBI SHR / BJS):** Approximately 50% of U.S. homicides go uncleared without known offender demographics, causing BJS to suspend official population-standardized homicide *offending* rate CIs; the ~7–10x male-to-female offending ratio is well established historically but lacks a formal sampling 95% CI.
10. **`household_firearm_homicide_victimization` (OR = 2.00, 95% CI 1.56–3.02; Anglemyer et al., 2014):** Measures homicide *victimization* of household members rather than homicide *perpetration* by the index patient; while clinically vital for domestic/IPV lethality counseling, it must not be conflated with an individual patient's general violence perpetration risk.
